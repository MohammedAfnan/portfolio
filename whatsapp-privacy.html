Work / Privacy messaging, hackathon concept

When WhatsApp’s privacy notice put the trust of 2 billion users at risk, I redesigned the message to protect retention.

The update was about business chats. Most people use WhatsApp to talk to family and friends, and many read the notice as a change to their private messages. I designed three short screens that answer that worry first and let people choose business features for themselves.

Problem

A policy about business chats read like a change to everyone’s private messages.

In January 2021 WhatsApp asked its users to accept an updated policy or lose access over time. Many people saw a line about sharing data with Facebook and assumed their personal chats were part of it. Signal and Telegram saw large jumps in downloads that month. Personal chats were not part of the change.

2014, the acquisition

Facebook bought WhatsApp. WhatsApp told users that privacy would stay at the centre of the product.

2016, a 30-day window

Phone numbers started being shared with Facebook. Users had 30 days to opt out.

2021, no opt-out

The new policy came with a deadline and no choice. Agree, or slowly lose access.

Four audiences, one message:People chatting with friends and familyCustomers messaging a business now and thenSellers running a business on WhatsAppBusinesses using WhatsApp and Facebook tools

What I found

The people least affected had the most reason to worry, because the notice didn’t tell them apart.

The changes were mostly for the fourth group. The first group is the largest, and the notice spoke to everyone the same way. It also left open the questions people were asking. Can WhatsApp read my messages? Does Facebook see my chats? What changes for me?

Rules I set for the message

Five rules every screen had to follow.

The P numbers point to the privacy by design principles I worked from.

Answer the worry firstReassurance comes before any legal text.
Plain wordsShort sentences a first-time reader can follow.
Privacy as the defaultP2. Nobody agrees to terms they don’t need.
Say encryption out loudP3. End-to-end security, stated in everyday words.
Leave the choice with the userP7. Business features are a setting the user owns.

Screen one: reassurance

Start with what isn’t changing.

The first screen is for most people, the ones who use WhatsApp for personal chats. It tells them their messages are still end-to-end encrypted and that WhatsApp can’t read them.

Screen two: business chats

Business chats get a small, clear label.

The policy only touched conversations with businesses, so the chat list treats them as their own kind of chat.

Screen three: a setting you own

Business messaging becomes opt-in, with privacy on by default.

Anyone who wants business features turns them on, reads what that means and carries on. Everyone else changes nothing.

How it compares

WhatsApp’s own fix made several of the same calls.

WhatsApp rolled out its response between February and May 2021, with an in-app banner, a staged explanation and a clear line that personal chats can’t be read. A jury picked my entry while that rollout was still going. I didn’t have their legal team or their scale. Where I could make the same calls, I landed close to theirs and went a little further on choice.

Outcome

A jury pick, and an idea that still needs testing.

How I counted

Audiences: from tracing WhatsApp’s policy history and who each change reached.

Screens: the original notice was one screen of legal text with one button. The proposal spreads it across three screens, each with one idea.

Result: picked as the winner by the UX Hack jury in April 2021. Nothing here was measured with real users.

What I’d do differently

Two things I’d change next time.

Test the copy

My screens assumed plain words calm people better than legal ones. It’s a safe bet, but I’d run a few versions through quick comprehension tests before choosing.

Spend less of the sprint on research

Mapping four audiences from the policy history took most of my time. With AI tools that’s a few hours now, and I’d put the saved time into testing.
