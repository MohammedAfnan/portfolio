Problem

CISOs often had a few minutes before a board call, and the dashboard gave them six widgets to piece together.

Too much to read

Scores, scanner coverage, SLA timers and the exposure funnel each told part of the story. Nobody joined them up.

Answers lived outside

Threat questions went to search engines, vendor reports and chat threads, then came back as screenshots.

Insight never became work

Finding a risk was one tool. Tagging, assigning and ticketing it was another, done by hand.

Two users, one feature:CISO needs the story in 30 secondsSOC analyst needs the evidence and the fix

Rules I set for the AI

Five rules I used to check every line the AI wrote.

SpecificCounts and names in every line. No paragraphs.
SourcedEvery claim links to the data behind it.
BalancedGood news next to bad, or nobody trusts it.
Honest about itselfShows when it ran and that it can be wrong.
Ends in a next stepEvery answer offers a question or an action.

Feature one: AI summary

A short summary above the widgets that takes about 30 seconds to read, with room to go deeper.

The summary reads scores, SLA timers, scanner coverage and the exposure funnel, then writes what changed and what to fix first.

Feature two: Ask Vigil

People can ask in plain words and get back a table, a chart and a suggested next step.

Analysts used to leave the product to research threats. Now the question, the evidence and the follow-up live in one thread. Try the tabs on the second screen.

Feature three: promptbooks

Promptbooks let anyone on the team rerun a senior analyst’s investigation with one click.

Every new threat actor or CVE got the same five or six questions from our best analysts. Juniors skipped steps or wrote the prompts differently, so no two reports looked alike. Promptbooks save the sequence once. Anyone runs it with one input and gets the same structure back.

Expert steps, sharedA senior analyst’s method becomes something a new hire can run on day one.
Same shape every timeEvery actor profile has the same sections, so reports compare side by side.
Type it onceThe threat actor goes in one field. A variable fills it into every prompt.
Nothing gets skippedThe book runs every step in order, even on a busy Monday.

Feature four: answer to action

Each answer can lead straight into the work of fixing the problem.

Create task suite opens a drawer the AI has already filled in. The analyst checks the scope, edits one field and saves.

Decision

One assistant that appears in three places, depending on what the person is doing.

I explored where the co-pilot should live. Each placement serves a different moment, so they share one engine and hand off to each other.

Full page

Best for
Open research, like threat trends across an industry.
Trade-off
Takes the user away from the page they were on.

Side panel

Best for
Questions about the list or dashboard on screen, with that context passed in.
Trade-off
Narrow width. Wide tables scroll or expand to full page.

Inline summary

Best for
Leaders who will never open a chat window.
Trade-off
Fixed questions. Links out to the panel for anything else.

Keep all three on one engine. The summary hands off to the panel, and the panel expands to the full page. The user never repeats their question.

Outcome

Teams could move from reading the dashboard to acting on it much sooner.

How I counted

Steps, before: research the threat outside the product, open the exposure list, build the filter, select matches, add tags, assign a workspace, raise a ticket in another tool. After: ask, press Create task suite, press Save and run.

Prompts: the threat actor book has five prompts. Before, an analyst typed each one with the actor name. Now they type the name once and press Run.

Fields: name, three filter values, tags, workspace and ticket were typed by hand. Now the AI drafts all of them and the analyst checks the name.

Reading time: the brief is 64 words. At an average adult reading speed that is under 20 seconds.

What I’d do differently

Looking back, there are three things I’d do differently.

Test with real board decks earlier

I’d put the summary in front of CISOs next to their own board slides before the layout was final.

Ship answer feedback on day one

Helpful or not, on every answer, so quality has a signal from the first week.

Design “I don’t know” as carefully as the right answer

A clear no-answer with a next step builds more trust than a confident guess.
